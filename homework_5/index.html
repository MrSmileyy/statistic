Theoretical Concepts, Interpretation & Causality Discussion

1. Statistical & Probabilistic Independence P(X = xi, F = yj) = P(X = xi) ⋅ P(F = yj)

Two categorical variables X and F are statistically independent if and only if their joint probability distribution factors into the product of their marginal distributions for every pair of categories: P(X = xi, F = yj) = P(X = xi) P(F = yj). Equivalently, knowing the value of X provides zero information about F, meaning the conditional distributions are identical across all rows: P(F = yj | X = xi) = P(F = yj). Under this null hypothesis H0, the expected cell frequency is Eij = (Ri ⋅ Cj) / N.

Theoretical Guide: Contingency Tables & Chi-Square Independence

1. Marginal & Conditional Frequencies

  • Marginal Frequencies: Row totals Ri = ∑j Oij and column totals Cj = ∑i Oij describe the univariate distribution of each variable separately.
  • Conditional Frequencies: P(F = yj | X = xi) = Oij / Ri measures how the distribution of F shifts within each subgroup of X.

2. Expected Frequencies & Pearson's Chi-Square

Under independence H0, expected counts are Eij = (Ri ⋅ Cj) / N. Pearson's statistic measures the total normalized discrepancy between observed and expected counts:
 χ² = ∑i=1r ∑j=1c (Oij - Eij)² / Eij
Asymptotically, χ² follows a Chi-Square distribution with df = (r - 1)(c - 1) degrees of freedom. The exact p-value is computed via the regularized upper incomplete gamma function Q(df/2, χ²/2).

3. Effect Size (Cramér's V) & Causality

Because χ² scales linearly with sample size N, even tiny deviations yield tiny p-values when N is large. Cramér's V = √(χ² / (N ⋅ min(r-1, c-1))) normalizes association strength to [0, 1]. Finally, rejecting H0 establishes statistical association, never causation—hidden confounders, reverse causality, or selection bias can induce strong dependence without any direct causal mechanism.
